# Offer design, pricing and conversion for a productized web-design service (solo side business, Slovenia)

Research date: 2026-10-31. Most sources are US-based and quote USD. Adjust them for Slovenia, where another researcher covers competitor prices and subsidies. Tags used below: **[OLDER]** marks sources from before 2023. **[VENDOR]** marks a company writing about a product or service it sells. **[SECONDARY]** marks a summary or aggregator rather than the original study. "Jure" means the user (sites.jurelabs.si, popoldanski s.p., evenings and weekends only, Vercel + Supabase "factory", three packages plus a configurator that produces a lead).

## Q1. Which offer frameworks are proven for productized services, and what do they concretely recommend (naming, bonuses, guarantees, scarcity/urgency, risk reversal)?

### Takeaway
The frameworks agree on one recipe. First productize: one written scope, one published price, one repeatable process (Casel-style). Then raise perceived value without adding custom work, following Hormozi's value equation: make the dream outcome vivid, raise the client's belief that it will happen (proof, guarantee), cut the wait, and cut the client's effort. Finally stack cheap-to-deliver bonuses, add a *conditional* guarantee, and add honest capacity scarcity. Designjoy is the clearest solo proof that this works. It runs one plan, an explicit exclusions list, a one-request-at-a-time queue, "pause or cancel anytime" and a short partial-refund window.

### Cited Findings
**Hormozi, *$100M Offers* (book 2021 [OLDER]; secondary summaries [SECONDARY])**
- Value equation: Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement) ÷ (Time Delay × Effort & Sacrifice) — [EcomCrew summary](https://www.ecomcrew.com/100m-offers-book-summary/); [SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/100m-offers/summary/)
- "People pay for certainty." Perceived likelihood matters even when the real effort is unchanged. Perceived time delay is shortened by showing small wins along the way — [EcomCrew summary](https://www.ecomcrew.com/100m-offers-book-summary/)
- A "Grand Slam Offer" has four parts: an attractive promotion (the hook), an unmatchable value proposition (the stack), a premium price and an unbeatable guarantee. It is built to be hard to compare with anything else on the market — [SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/100m-offers/summary/)
- Build steps: (1) identify the dream outcome; (2) list every problem and limiting belief; (3) turn each problem into a named solution; (4) choose delivery vehicles; (5) trim to the highest-value, lowest-cost items; (6) bundle into one stack — [EcomCrew summary](https://www.ecomcrew.com/100m-offers-book-summary/)
- Enhancers, all from the same summary — [EcomCrew](https://www.ecomcrew.com/100m-offers-book-summary/):
  - **Scarcity.** For services: caps on total clients, on growth rate or on cohort size. "It's important that you always sell out and tell people you sold out."
  - **Urgency.** "Deadlines drive decisions." Options: cohorts, seasonal deadlines, price or bonus expiry, and "exploding" opportunities whose value drops with delay.
  - **Bonuses.** "A single offer is less than the same offer broken into its component parts and stacked as bonuses." Always name, price and justify each bonus.
  - **Guarantees, four types:** *unconditional* (pay first, try, refund); *conditional* (has terms, and should be stronger than a plain money-back guarantee); *anti-guarantee* ("all sales are final", for things that cannot be given back); *implied/performance* (revenue share, profit share, bonuses). Guarantees can be stacked.
  - **Naming.** "Change the packaging, not the offer." The headline formula is a magnetic reason + a specific avatar + a goal (the dream outcome) + a time interval, and "the shorter and punchier, the better."
- Pricing advice: "The more niche the product, the higher you can charge." "Price it in a way that it stings a little when they buy." Avoid winning on price cuts and aim for a "category of one" — [EcomCrew summary](https://www.ecomcrew.com/100m-offers-book-summary/)

**Productized services (Brian Casel and others)**
- A productized service is one scope, one price and one repeatable process, with price, scope, process and result fixed up front. Prices are published, with no "starting at" or "it depends." Scope is written so a stranger could verify it. "A productized service with undefined scope is a fixed-price custom engagement." — secondary guides: [ManyRequests](https://www.manyrequests.com/blog/productized-service-guide), [t2informatik](https://t2informatik.de/en/smartpedia/productized-service/), [Agiled](https://agiled.app/hub/agencies/how-to-build-a-productized-service) [SECONDARY; these phrases came via a search summary, so which phrase is on which page is uncertain]
- Brian Casel, widely credited with popularizing the model, built **Restaurant Engine**, a productized web-design service for one niche (restaurants), and Audience Ops (2015). Restaurant Engine is a direct analog to a niche website factory — [Startups for the Rest of Us, ep. 208](https://startupsfortherestofus.com/episodes/episode-208-how-to-productize-your-service-with-brian-casel); [Mixergy course notes](https://mixergy.com/course-cheat-sheet-productize-your-service/) [OLDER]

**Designjoy (primary source, homepage fetched 2026-10-31)** — [Designjoy](https://www.designjoy.co/)
- One plan, "Monthly Club", at **$4,995/month**. The page shows **$5,995 struck through** with the label "Lifetime Discount – Limited Time", which works as an anchor plus urgency.
- Included: "One request at a time", "Avg. 48 hour delivery", unlimited brands, Webflow development, unlimited stock photos, up to 2 users.
- Clients can queue unlimited requests, but they are delivered one by one. Large projects arrive "in batches… every 24–48 hours". Onboarding runs through a Trello board about an hour after subscribing, with no sales call needed.
- "Pause or cancel anytime." Billing runs on 31-day cycles, and unused days carry over when paused.
- Risk reversal: "Not loving it after a week? Get 75% back, no questions asked." There are no refunds after week one, and completed work is never refunded. Revisions continue "until you're 100% satisfied."
- Scarcity: a "limited roster of clients at a time."
- Explicit exclusions: 3D, animated graphics, video, document design, complex packaging, extensive print and InDesign.
- One designer, with no outsourcing or extra hires.
- Revenue figures conflict and are unverified:
  - ~$145k/month with ~20 active clients in June 2024 — [onepage-research aggregator](https://onepage-research.sliplane.app/products/designjoy)
  - "$3.1M" ARR as of Oct 2024 — [Starter Story](https://www.starterstory.com/businesses/design-joy/monetization)
  - "0 to $120k/month with no paid advertising" — [Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/how-designjoy-got-from-0-to-120k-month-with-no-paid-advertising-a31375fce0)
  - [SECONDARY]

**Website-as-a-Service (WaaS) [VENDOR]**
- We.Inc guide (updated 23–24 Oct 2026) — [We.Inc](https://we.inc/blog/website-as-a-service-subscription-websites):
  - Published WaaS offers reviewed in Sept 2026 cluster around **$99–$149/month** for a small-business site.
  - Clients pay $0 or a small setup fee up front.
  - A **12-month minimum** is common. Some providers use 3–6 months, then month-to-month.
  - Usually included: design, hosting, SSL, small monthly edits and support. Extras: more pages, campaigns, e-commerce and buyouts.

### Inferences
- **How the value equation applies to Jure's factory.** Automation is Jure's biggest lever because it cuts *time delay* (preview in hours, launch in days) and *client effort* ("answer 10 questions and send 5 photos; we write the text"). Those are the two denominators of the value equation, so automation raises perceived value without raising Jure's hours. *Perceived likelihood* is the weak point for a new brand. It is best raised with a live preview of the client's own site, niche demo sites, a performance guarantee and transparent terms (see Q3 and Q6).
- **Niche the packaging, not the product.** Following Hormozi ("the more niche… the higher you can charge") and Casel's Restaurant Engine, Jure can keep one technical template system and sell it as several niche-named offers on separate landing pages, e.g. "Spletna stran za frizerske salone", "…za apartmaje in turizem", "…za obrtnike". Only copy, demo content and default modules change, such as booking for salons or multilingual pages for apartments.

#### Copyable offer skeleton (illustrative; calibrate prices with the competitor research)
- **Name (Hormozi formula: reason + avatar + goal + time):** "Nova spletna stran za [frizerske salone], ki prinaša rezervacije — v 7 dneh" ("A new website for [hair salons] that brings in bookings — in 7 days"). Optional magnetic reason: "Jesenska akcija za ustanovne stranke" ("Autumn offer for founding clients").
- **Core:** a fast, mobile-first site from the niche template, with the client's texts written for them (AI-drafted, client-approved), domain, SSL, hosting and a contact/booking call-to-action.
- **Stacked bonuses.** Each is named and given a reference value. Each is cheap to automate and solves the client's *next* problem:
  - Google Business Profile setup or optimization
  - a QR "leave us a review" print file
  - a review-request message template or automation
  - a launch social-post kit
  - basic SEO and LocalBusiness structured data
  - a monthly automated visitor report
  - 1–3 months of the care plan free
- **Guarantees, stacked and conditional (details in Q3):** "See your site before you pay"; "Live within 7 days of receiving your content, or 3 months of care are free"; "Mobile PageSpeed 90+ at launch, or we fix it free."
- **Honest scarcity:** "Because I build every site personally alongside my day job, I take at most N new projects per month." This is true for a side business, so it is credible and does not need to be faked.
- **Honest urgency:** founding-client pricing for the first 10 clients in exchange for a testimonial and a Google review. Seasonal deadlines also work, e.g. "Live before the summer season" for tourism.

### Gaps
- I could not access the book text itself. The guarantee taxonomy and quotes come from secondary summaries; one fetch, of Dean Yeong's notes, failed with HTTP 503.
- I found no controlled evidence on how much each Hormozi enhancer (bonuses, scarcity, naming) lifts conversion for web services. His framework is practitioner doctrine, not experimental evidence.
- Casel's own canonical definition of a productized service was not retrieved. The definitions above are secondary paraphrases.
- Designjoy's revenue figures are inconsistent across secondary sources (~$1.3M to $3.1M/yr).

## Q2. Packaging and pricing: number of tiers, anchoring/decoys, one-time vs subscription vs hybrid, "no upfront + 12-month minimum", annual prepay, à-la-carte add-ons — evidence on conversion and churn, and workable price points

### Takeaway
Pricing experts (Blair Enns, Jonathan Stark) consistently recommend **three options with a high anchor**. However, the academic "decoy tier" effect is weak or fails to replicate in realistic settings, so every tier should be a genuine choice and none should exist only as a dummy. The strongest dataset found, The Admin Bar 2026 survey of 622 agency owners and freelancers, ties profitability strongly to **recurring revenue ≥25%** and **project prices ≥$5k**. It finds that projects under $1k are the riskiest. The "€0 upfront + monthly with a 12-month minimum" model is common at $99–$149/month in the US and from about €30/month in Europe. It only pays back quickly when the build takes very few hours, which favors Jure's automation. I found **no independent churn or conversion data** for website subscriptions.

### Cited Findings
**Three options and anchoring (practitioner doctrine)**
- Blair Enns, *Pricing Creativity* (2018) [OLDER]:
  - Offering options is a core rule. He claims three options raise the share of positive outcomes (win rate) by about half. This is the author's claim, not a published study.
  - He recommends leading with the most expensive option as a high anchor — [David Airey on Pricing Creativity](https://archive.davidairey.com/dealing-with-designers/pricing-creativity-blair-enns/); [Impact Pricing podcast ep. 27](https://impactpricing.com/podcast/ep27-blair-enns-pricing-creativity-applied-to-products/)
- Enns: a single price invites comparison with competitors, while three options let the client weigh levels of ambition, risk and investment — [Teamwork masterclass summary](https://www.teamwork.com/blog/value-masterclass-key-takeaways/)
- Jonathan Stark: offer three tiers of increasing value, each at a fixed final price, which anchors buyers toward higher-value options. "It's not about scope — it's about benefits and outcomes." — [Content Snare](https://contentsnare.com/value-based-pricing-jonathan-stark/); [B2B Launcher #090](https://b2blauncher.com/?p=3049) [OLDER]

**Decoy (attraction) effect: weak real-world evidence**
- Frederick, Lee & Baskin (2014, *Journal of Marketing Research*) and Yang & Lynn (2014) ran large replications and failed to find the decoy effect under realistic conditions. It appeared mainly in stylized lab tasks with numeric attributes. Devine et al. (2025) found a real-world decoy effect in UK wine purchases but call it a "proof of principle" with small effects — [Devine et al. 2025 (PDF)](https://otto.lab.mcgill.ca/papers/devine_et_al_2025a.pdf)
- Frequent shoppers were less sensitive to decoys in a large real-world shopping dataset — [IJPDS](https://ijpds.org/article/view/2286)

**Business-health evidence by pricing model and price level** — [The Admin Bar 2026 survey](https://theadminbar.com/2026-survey/). Sample: 622 WordPress agency owners and freelancers in 51 countries, surveyed March 2026 about 2025 business. Median team size is 1, i.e. owner only.
- **Recurring revenue share** (→ "rarely/never profitable" / "consistently profitable"):

  | Recurring share | Rarely/never profitable | Consistently profitable |
  |---|---|---|
  | 0% | 58.3% | 25.0% |
  | 1–24% | 16.7% | 29.7% |
  | 25–49% | 9.7% | 47.4% |
  | 50–74% | 4.5% | 47.8% |
  | 75%+ | 6.2% | 46.9% |

- **Typical project price** (→ consistently profitable / rarely-never profitable):

  | Project price | Consistently profitable | Rarely/never profitable |
  |---|---|---|
  | $100–999 | 25.0% | 37.5% |
  | $1,000–2,499 | 30.0% | 13.6% |
  | $2,500–4,999 | 36.8% | 11.8% |
  | $5,000–9,999 | 56.8% | 2.7% |
  | $10,000–19,999 | 50.0% | 3.1% |

- **Pricing model** (→ consistently profitable / $100k+ revenue / grew in 2025):

  | Model | Consistently profitable | $100k+ revenue | Grew in 2025 |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Retainer | 51.2% | 59.0% | 56.6% |
  | Productized | 44.4% | 51.1% | 55.6% |
  | Value-based | 46.2% | 27.3% | 50.0% |
  | Fixed-price | 36.4% | 35.5% | 47.4% |
  | Hourly | 39.8% | 34.9% | 42.2% |

  The survey itself warns that causality may run the other way: healthier agencies may be the ones that choose retainers.

**Website-as-a-Service terms and economics [VENDOR]** — [We.Inc](https://we.inc/blog/website-as-a-service-subscription-websites)
- Offers cluster at $99–$149/month. One example charges **$99/month for 12 months, then $39/month** once the build is paid off.
- "Some European providers start near 30 euros a month for simpler sites."
- **Break-even math.** Assumptions: build time valued at $60/h, platform cost $20/month per client, price $99/month, so $79/month kept.
  - 40-hour build ($2,400) → break-even in **month 31**
  - 15-hour build ($900) → **month 12**
  - 5-hour build ($300) → **month 4**
  - Formula: build cost ÷ monthly amount kept.
- It suggests a setup fee for slow builds and a buyout fee to exit inside the minimum term.
- Ownership: the client owns the domain, logo, text and photos; the provider owns the platform and design system. The domain should be registered in the client's name.
- It gives no churn figures.

**Real-world WaaS examples (search snippets)**
- Northwoods Web Designs: plans at $79/month and $289/month, each with a 12-month minimum. After the term there is no cancellation fee; leaving early triggers a pro-rated lump sum of $2,000 — [Northwoods](https://northwoodswebdesigns.com/p/pay-monthly-website-design.php)
- It's Paul (Canada): $150 + HST/month with a 12-month minimum, covering up to 5 pages, hosting and ongoing edits — [itspaul.ca](https://www.itspaul.ca/subscription-based-website-design-for-small-businesses/)

**Annual vs monthly churn (not web-services data)**
- Consumer and e-commerce subscription benchmark blogs (2026) put monthly-plan churn at about 5–8% per month. They claim annual plans churn far less, with one claiming annual subscribers churn ~40% less — [Flowium](https://flowium.com/blog/subscription-churn-rate/); [EightX](https://eightx.co/blog/average-subscription-churn-rate-by-category) [VENDOR; I could not confirm which page carries which figure]

### Inferences
- **Tiers.** Keep **three visible tiers** and design the middle one as the target. Add a fourth "Po meri / Custom, od €X" ("from €X") card priced high. It works as a credible anchor, as Enns recommends, and also filters out custom work Jure does not want. Because decoy effects are weak in realistic settings, don't build a dominated dummy tier. Make each tier fit a real segment, e.g. a one-page business card site / a booking-focused site / a multilingual or tourism site.
- **Payment structure, "pay once or pay monthly" toggle.** Show each tier's price as either:
  - (a) a one-time fee + an optional care plan, or
  - (b) **€0 or a small setup fee + monthly with a 12-month minimum**, rolling month-to-month after that, with a clear buyout price.

  The monthly route should cost more over 24 months than the one-time route (a financing premium), as in We.Inc's "$99 → $39 after payback" pattern. This gives the cash-poor small business the irresistible "no upfront cost" option without forcing it on everyone.
- **The break-even math favors automation.** At We.Inc's numbers, a 5-hour build pays back in ~4 months and a 40-hour build in ~31. Jure should therefore offer the no-upfront plan *only* on templated tiers where his own build time is a few hours. Custom work should require a setup fee or a one-time price.
- **Avoid the sub-€1k custom trap.** The Admin Bar data shows the $100–999 project band has the worst profitability (37.5% rarely/never profitable). If Jure sells at low Slovenian price points, those prices must be paired with near-zero build hours and a recurring component. Otherwise he should differentiate on speed, preview and guarantees rather than on price.
- **Annual prepay (inference; no source found on the discount size).** Offer the care plan annually at "12 months for the price of 10". The general subscription data suggests annual billing lowers churn, but this is not verified for web services.
- **À-la-carte add-ons.** Price every non-core feature as a module in the configurator, e.g. extra language, extra page, booking integration, payment-link "mini shop", logo, copywriting, photo session through a partner. That keeps the base tiers clean, and the configurator turns add-ons into instant upsells.

### Gaps
- I found **no independent data** on conversion or churn for website subscriptions vs one-off builds vs hybrids in web services. Operator blogs give terms but not retention.
- I found no source on typical annual-prepay discount levels for web care plans.
- European and Slovenian SMB price points are out of scope here; another researcher covers them. The only European figure found was "from ~€30/month" for simple WaaS sites (We.Inc, vendor).
- The Admin Bar sample is WordPress-focused and global, and its profitability links are correlations.

## Q3. Which guarantees are safe for a solo operator, raise conversion, and do not create unbounded work — and how should they be scoped?

### Takeaway
Guarantee only what is **fully in your control**: time to preview, time to launch *after the client delivers content*, technical quality scores, and transparent exit and ownership. Make them **conditional** on client actions. Pay out in low-cost currency, such as free months of care or credits, or in a **capped partial refund within a short window**, like Designjoy's "75% back in week one". Retail evidence suggests lenient money and effort terms raise purchases more than they raise returns. Outcome guarantees (rankings, leads) and truly unlimited revisions on a fixed-price build are the dangerous ones.

### Cited Findings
- Hormozi: conditional guarantees should be stronger than a plain money-back promise, and guarantees can be stacked — [EcomCrew summary](https://www.ecomcrew.com/100m-offers-book-summary/) [OLDER/SECONDARY]
- Designjoy's guarantee design — [Designjoy](https://www.designjoy.co/):
  - 75% refund within the first week, "no questions asked"
  - no refunds after week one, and completed work is never refunded
  - "unlimited" revisions bounded by a *one-request-at-a-time* queue, so throughput (and Jure's hours) stays fixed
  - explicit exclusions list
- Meta-analysis by Janakiraman, Syrdal & Freling (2016, *Journal of Retailing*, ~21–22 studies) on return-policy leniency — [UT Dallas news](https://news.utdallas.edu/?p=11491); [UTA via Cision](https://news.cision.com/university-of-texas-at-arlington/r/uta-marketing-study-shows-lenient-return-policy-may-increase-sales,c2032384) [OLDER; retail goods, not services]:
  - Lenient return policies raise purchases more than they raise returns.
  - *Money* and *effort* leniency were most associated with more purchases.
  - Longer time windows *reduced* returns (an endowment effect).
  - *Scope* leniency increased returns.
  - The lead author warns against being "too lenient."
- Scope edit allowances precisely, e.g. "up to 2 content changes a month" — [We.Inc](https://we.inc/blog/website-as-a-service-subscription-websites) [VENDOR]
- Fast sites convert better: Portent 2022 found a 1-second site converts ~3× better than a 5-second one (B2B) — [Portent](https://www.portent.com/blog/analytics/research-site-speed-hurting-everyone.htm). This gives a speed guarantee real client value.

### Inferences
**A guarantee menu ranked by safety for a side business (the cost estimates are my own)**

| Guarantee (example wording) | Type | Jure's exposure | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Vidite svojo stran, preden plačate" ("See your site before you pay"): free automated preview; pay a deposit only if you like it | Risk-free trial, pre-payment | Low if fully automated: compute plus a few minutes per lead | Gate it to qualified leads (business name, VAT/registration number or Google Business Profile link, phone). Limit to one preview per business. |
| "Live within 7 days after we receive your content, or your first 3 months of care are free" | Conditional, non-cash payout | Low: the payout costs hosting only | The clock starts only when the onboarding form, logo, photos and text approval are complete. Weekends and holidays are counted honestly. |
| "Mobile PageSpeed ≥90 and Core Web Vitals pass at launch, or we fix it free" | Conditional, performance (in your control) | Very low on a static or Next.js stack on Vercel | Exclude third-party embeds the client insists on, such as booking widgets and chat. |
| "Don't like the design direction? Get your deposit back" (only before build starts) | Conditional refund, capped | Bounded: the deposit is refunded only at the preview stage | This mirrors Designjoy's "short window + partial" logic. |
| "No lock-in after 12 months; your domain and content are yours; export on request" | Risk reversal and trust | Little extra work; it does mean clients can leave freely after 12 months | Pairs with the WaaS ownership split (We.Inc). |
| "Unlimited small text fixes in the first 30 days after launch, one request at a time" | Bounded "unlimited" | Bounded by the queue | Designjoy's queue logic, limited in time. |

**Avoid:**
- "More customers or your money back" and "#1 on Google". Results depend on the client's market and actions and on Google.
- Unlimited revisions on a one-off fixed price with no time limit.
- Refunds after launch, once the work is delivered.

**Terms that keep the work bounded (copyable clauses):**
- **Content deadline.** The client provides content within 10 days of the deposit. After that, Jure either launches with AI-drafted placeholder copy the client can edit later, or the project goes "on hold" and later restart is subject to capacity and a rebooking fee. Final payment is due at launch either way.
- **Revision definition.** Each tier includes two rounds of *consolidated* feedback, meaning one written list per round, returned within 3 working days. A layout or feature outside the selected package is a *change request*, not a revision.
- **Change-request menu.** Publish prices for extra page, extra language, extra form, integration and custom section. Anything not on the menu goes to the "Custom" tier.
- **Monthly edit allowance on care plans.** "Up to N small content changes/month, one request at a time, done within 3 working days; unused edits don't roll over."
- **Response-time promise that fits a day job.** E.g. "replies within one working day, evenings included." Be honest rather than promising 1-hour support.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled study on guarantees for **web-design services** specifically. The leniency meta-analysis covers retail goods.
- No data on claim rates, i.e. how often clients invoke refunds or time guarantees, for productized web services.
- B2B vs B2C consumer-law limits on guarantee wording, and the fact that a Slovenian s.p. is a natural person, should be checked by the legal researcher.

## Q4. Recurring revenue: which care plans and add-ons do web freelancers sell, at what prices, and with what retention?

### Takeaway
Freelancer care plans typically run **$50–150/month**, with some up to $300. Agency care plans run **$100–500/month**, where a "protection" layer (updates, backups, monitoring, a few edits) sits at the bottom and "growth" layers (SEO, content, reporting) sit above. These are US vendor figures. Recurring revenue is the strongest correlate of agency profitability in the 2026 Admin Bar data. **No credible public retention or churn figures** for care plans were found. Jure should therefore design for visible monthly value and low support load, and measure his own churn.

### Cited Findings
- Price bands, all from [Ketchup Consulting](https://ketchupconsulting.com/insights/website-maintenance-plan-cost-2026/) (July 2026) [VENDOR]:
  - DIY tool stack $30–75/month; freelancer retainers $50–150/month; agency care plans $100–500/month; enterprise $1,000+/month
  - "Protection" layer: software updates, security monitoring, off-site backups, uptime monitoring and a monthly edit allotment
  - "Growth" layer: SEO health monitoring, scheduled content, performance tuning and plain-English reporting
  - Stage bands: Protection $75–150, Growth $250–350 (elsewhere $250–500), Compounding $450–600/month
  - Emergency malware cleanup costs "$600 or more per incident"
  - Professionally written SEO articles cost $150–500 each, historically
- Freelancers charge $50–150/hour or $50–300/month for maintenance — [Venbit](https://venbit.com/articles/how-much-do-freelancers-charge-for-website-maintenance) [VENDOR; search snippet]
- Recurring revenue vs profitability: 0% recurring → 58.3% rarely/never profitable; 25–49% → 9.7% — [The Admin Bar 2026](https://theadminbar.com/2026-survey/)
- Client concentration, same survey: when the top-3 clients make up <25% of revenue, 51.0% of businesses are consistently profitable, vs 29.4% when they make up ≥75% — [The Admin Bar 2026](https://theadminbar.com/2026-survey/)
- WaaS plans bundle hosting, SSL, small monthly edits and support. "Support hours, not platform cost, usually break first, so cap edits before reaching about 25 clients." — [We.Inc](https://we.inc/blog/website-as-a-service-subscription-websites) [VENDOR]
- Example WaaS tiers — [We.Inc](https://we.inc/blog/website-as-a-service-subscription-websites):
  - Starter: up to 5 pages and small edits
  - Growth: more pages, a booking or quote form and a monthly report
  - Premium: priority changes, campaign landing pages and a quarterly review

### Inferences
- **Pitch the stack as a strength.** A static or Jamstack site on Vercel (with Supabase only where dynamic data is needed) has no WordPress plugins to patch and a far smaller attack surface. The WordPress "protection" work behind many care plans, such as $600 malware cleanups, mostly disappears. Jure can sell care on *outcomes and convenience* ("we handle everything; you call or email one person") rather than on maintenance chores. That keeps his time per client very low.
- **Suggested care-plan ladder (illustrative):**
  - **Osnovno / Basic:** hosting, SSL, backups, uptime monitoring, an automated monthly visitor report and 1 small edit/month
  - **Rast / Growth:** + 3 edits/month, an automated review-request flow (QR + email/SMS template), a quarterly Google Business Profile and SEO check, and seasonal content swaps
  - **Premium:** + one landing page per quarter, multilingual updates and priority queue
  - Sell it annually ("2 months free") and include the first 1–3 months in the build price as a bonus, so clients experience its value before paying.
- **Automatable "growth" add-ons suited to Jure's hours:**
  - automated monthly analytics email (Vercel Analytics or Plausible → PDF/HTML report)
  - review-generation kit
  - GBP post scheduling
  - AI-drafted blog or news posts that need *client approval*, so quality and liability stay with the client
  - uptime and SSL alerts
  - domain and e-mail management
- **Retention levers (inference):**
  - make value visible with the monthly report
  - offer **pause** instead of cancel, as Designjoy does
  - put the domain in the client's name but keep DNS management convenient
  - add annual prepay
  - avoid relying on a few big clients (Admin Bar concentration data)

## Q5. Configurator and inquiry-to-sale funnel: instant price vs request a quote, public prices, multi-step forms, instant proposals, call vs self-checkout with deposit, follow-up, speed-to-lead

### Takeaway
The best available evidence comes mostly from B2B buyers or from vendors. It favors **transparent, self-serve pricing** and **instant response**:
- Gartner (2026): 67% of B2B buyers prefer a rep-free experience.
- TrustRadius (2022): 71% are more likely to buy if prices are on the website.
- HBR (2011): lead qualification odds collapse as response time grows.

Multi-step forms help when there are more than about five fields, but the big published uplifts are unverified vendor claims. For an operator with a day job, the practical answer is to **automate the first response** with an instant priced proposal plus an auto-generated preview. Offer **both** "reserve your slot with a deposit" and "book a 15-minute call."

### Cited Findings
- **Self-serve preference, Gartner 2026.** 67% of B2B buyers prefer a rep-free experience (646 buyers, surveyed Aug–Sep 2025), and 45% used AI during a recent purchase — [Gartner, Mar 2026](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-03-09-gartner-sales-survey-finds-67-percent-of-b2b-buyers-prefer-a-rep-free-experience)
- **Self-serve preference, Gartner 2025.** 61% preferred rep-free (632 buyers, Aug–Sep 2024), but buyers still want seller input for "fit" judgments, i.e. whether the product or service suits their needs — [Gartner, Jun 2025](https://gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-sales-survey-finds-61-percent-of-b2b-buyers-prefer-a-rep-free-buying-experience)
- **Price transparency, TrustRadius** (2022 survey of 2,185 tech buyers). 71% said readily available pricing on a vendor's website makes them more likely to buy. 81% want to find pricing on their own — [TrustRadius](https://solutions.trustradius.com/vendor-blog/tech-buyers-demand-honest-and-up-front-pricing-info/) [OLDER; B2B software buyers]
- **Price transparency, eMarketer.** 45% of B2B tech buyers name pricing transparency as the No. 1 change they want from vendors (survey year unclear) — [eMarketer](https://www.emarketer.com/content/b2b-tech-buyers-crave-pricing-transparency)
- **Price transparency, practitioner anecdotes.** Small-business owners on UK Business Forums report two effects. Publishing prices "filters out a LOT of time-wasters" and raises quote-to-sale conversion. Hiding prices can raise contact volume. A common middle ground is publishing a starting price or ranges — [UK Business Forums thread](https://www.ukbusinessforums.co.uk/threads/pricing-on-website.357328/post-2649701) [anecdotal]
- **Instant quote tools.** A 2007 MarketingSherpa case reports a self-service price-quote tool converting 48% of prospects into qualified leads — [MarketingSherpa](https://marketingsherpa.com/article/case-study/how-selfservice-price-quote-converts) [OLDER]. A calculator vendor reports one site's form submissions rising "over 30%" the month after adding a price-quote calculator — [Outgrow](https://outgrow.co/blog/price-quote-calculator/) [VENDOR; anecdote]
- **Multi-step forms.** Vendor blogs circulate a Formstack-attributed 13.9% (multi-step) vs 4.5% (single-page) conversion figure and an "86% better" claim. A comparison of *well-built* forms suggests a lift closer to ~14%. Field count matters most: multi-step tends to win beyond ~5 fields, and ≤4 fields should stay single-step — [Leadgen Economy](https://www.leadgen-economy.com/blog/multi-step-forms-conversion-optimization/); [Reform](https://reform.app/blog/single-page-vs-multi-step-forms-which-converts-better); [Orbit Forms](https://orbitforms.ai/blog/multi-step-forms-conversion-rate) [VENDOR; I could not trace a primary study or confirm which page holds which figure]
- **Speed-to-lead, HBR** (audit of 2,241 US companies). 37% responded within an hour and 23% never responded. Average response time was 42 hours. Firms that tried to contact a lead within an hour were ~7× as likely to qualify it as those who waited even one more hour, and >60× as likely as those who waited 24h+ — [HBR, "The Short Life of Online Sales Leads" (2011)](https://hbr.org/2011/03/the-short-life-of-online-sales-leads) [OLDER, but still the most-cited primary study]
- **Speed-to-lead, recent anecdote.** One founder reports a "900% conversion advantage just by answering leads faster" — [Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/how-we-found-a-900-conversion-advantage-just-by-answering-leads-faster-52d7a8aa8d) [anecdotal]
- **Self-checkout precedent.** Designjoy sells a $4,995/month service with no sales call: subscribe, then onboarding on Trello within about an hour — [Designjoy](https://www.designjoy.co/)

### Inferences
**Configurator design (copyable)**
1. **Step order:** business type/niche → main goal (calls, bookings, enquiries, presentation) → pages → features/add-ons → languages → timeline → *contact details last*.
2. **Show a live running price** from step 2 onward, with a one-time / monthly toggle. Hiding the price until a quote arrives works against the self-serve preference and the price-transparency evidence. Showing it also filters out low-budget leads before they cost Jure evening hours.
3. **Gate only the extras.** Use the email as the "key" to (a) send the full proposal PDF or link and (b) generate the personalized preview ("Pošljite mi ponudbo in predogled strani" — "Send me the proposal and a preview"). That captures the lead without hiding the price.
4. **Keep the contact step short:** name, email, phone (optional) and business name or website or Google Business Profile link. Track drop-off per step and A/B test, since the uplift numbers are vendor claims.

**Instant automated proposal (Supabase + Vercel)**
- Within about a minute of submission, email a unique proposal page containing:
  - the selected tier, add-ons and price (one-time and monthly)
  - the timeline, the guarantee terms and the scope/exclusions summary
  - 2–3 niche demo links and the FAQ
  - two CTAs: **"Rezerviraj termin — plačaj 30% avans" ("Reserve your slot, 30% deposit")** via Stripe, and **"15-min klic" ("15-minute call")** via a calendar link limited to evening slots
- Gartner's finding that buyers want seller input for fit decisions argues for keeping the call option. The rep-free preference and the Designjoy precedent argue for making checkout possible without one.

**Speed-to-lead for a day-job operator**
- Automation covers the "first touch within minutes" that HBR rewards: instant proposal email plus an SMS or email confirmation.
- Add a *human* touch the same evening, such as a short personal email or a 60-second Loom video walking through the preview.
- Commit publicly to a realistic SLA, e.g. "personal reply the same day, by 21:00."

**Follow-up sequence (inference; no data found)**
- Day 0: instant proposal
- Day 1: "your preview is ready" (if generated asynchronously)
- Day 3: a case study or demo from the same niche plus FAQ objections (price, content effort, ownership)
- Day 7: honest capacity note ("2 slots left this month"), only if true
- Day 14: a polite "close the file?" email
- Stop the sequence on reply or payment, and log every step in Supabase.

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous study of price display, configurators or instant quotes for **local SMB buyers of web services**. The evidence is B2B software (Gartner, TrustRadius), old (MarketingSherpa 2007) or vendor-sourced.
- No data on "deposit self-checkout" vs "book a call" conversion for small-business web projects.
- I verified no data on follow-up sequence length or timing for inbound service leads. Only cold-email benchmarks exist (see Q7).
- No recent primary speed-to-lead study was verified. Figures from 2021–2024 (Drift, RevenueHero, Velocify) appear only in vendor or secondary write-ups.

## Q6. Trust and proof for a new solo business without a big portfolio

### Takeaway
Local buyers lean heavily on reviews and increasingly on AI assistants. BrightLocal 2026 (US) found that 97% read reviews of local businesses, 68% will only use businesses rated 4+ stars, and 45% use generative AI for local recommendations, up from 6%. A new solo business can substitute **proof of capability** for a missing portfolio:
- a live preview of the prospect's *own* site
- niche demo sites
- live performance scores
- transparent terms and exclusions
- a visible local founder

It should also collect reviews and case studies from a **founding-client cohort** as fast as possible.

### Cited Findings
- BrightLocal Local Consumer Review Survey 2026, from secondary write-ups — [PinMeTo](https://www.pinmeto.com/news/brightlocal-local-consumer-review-survey-2026/); [BrightLocal local SEO stats hub](https://www.brightlocal.com/learn/local-seo/local-seo-statistics/) [SECONDARY; US sample]. Sample: 1,002 US adults, published Feb 2026.
  - 97% of consumers read reviews for local businesses; 41% "always" do, up from 29% a year earlier.
  - 68% will only use a business rated ≥4 stars, up from 55%.
  - 89% expect owners to respond to reviews.
  - Google's share of review use fell from 83% to 71%, and Apple Maps rose from 14% to 27%.
  - Use of ChatGPT or other gen-AI tools for local business recommendations rose from 6% (2025) to 45% (2026).
- Hormozi: "People pay for certainty." Perceived likelihood of achievement is a direct value driver — [EcomCrew summary](https://www.ecomcrew.com/100m-offers-book-summary/)
- Demo-first selling: build a rough version of the prospect's homepage before contacting them. The vendor claims a weekly sprint of 10 demos yields ~3–4 replies, 1–2 calls and ~1 deal, and warns that doing it at scale without personalization gets you flagged as spam — [SkipThePitch](https://skipthepitch.app/resources/demo-first-web-design-outreach) [VENDOR; unverified]
- Designjoy's trust devices — [Designjoy](https://www.designjoy.co/):
  - one named person
  - explicit inclusions and exclusions
  - a short refund window
  - a "limited roster"
  - a self-explanatory process (queue, 48-hour delivery, pause)

### Inferences
- **"Vidite stran, preden plačate" ("See your site before you pay") is Jure's strongest trust and conversion lever.** It also suits his automation. From the configurator answers (niche, name, colors, services, optional logo and photos), auto-generate a preview on a Vercel preview URL using the niche template, with AI-drafted Slovenian copy marked "osnutek" (draft). It directly raises "perceived likelihood" and cuts "time delay" in the value equation. Only use content the prospect submitted or explicitly authorized; scraping third-party data has GDPR implications for the legal researcher. Rate-limit previews and gate them to identifiable businesses to protect compute and time.
- **Demo gallery.** Publish 6–10 niche demo sites with clearly fictional brand names labelled "demo": e.g. salon, beauty, restaurant/gostilna, apartments, car service, physiotherapy, law office, construction/obrtnik. Show a live PageSpeed or Lighthouse badge on each. Clear labelling keeps demos honest rather than fake case studies.
- **Founding-client program.** Offer the first ~10 clients a discount in exchange for:
  - a written testimonial
  - a Google review
  - permission to publish a before/after case study with metrics (PageSpeed, enquiries)

  This creates honest urgency and fixes the empty-portfolio problem within a few months.
- **Founder and local identity.** Show Jure's photo, a short story ("I build sites in the evenings alongside my job, so I automated everything that doesn't need me"), the registered s.p. details, a local phone number and a realistic reply-time promise. Being transparent about the side business turns "small" into "personal and efficient."
- **Jure's own Google Business Profile.** Collect reviews from every client and respond to all of them; 89% of consumers expect responses.
- **AI search visibility as a selling point.** Given the rise in gen-AI local recommendations, structured data, a clear NAP (name, address, phone) and fast crawlable pages are credible benefits to include in every tier. That rise is US data; I found no Slovenian figure.

### Gaps
- I found no data measuring the conversion effect of "preview before you pay" for web services. The case for it rests on frameworks and analogies.
- BrightLocal figures are US data from secondary write-ups; I found no Slovenian or EU equivalent.
- I found no evidence on how much a founder photo or story lifts conversion for service sites. Nielsen Norman Group credibility research exists but was not retrieved here.

## Q7. Lead generation and referral mechanics suited to a side business (including a brief GDPR/cold-email note)

### Takeaway
In the 2026 Admin Bar survey, businesses using **proactive channels** (SEO, partnerships, community) were far more likely to exceed $100k revenue and to be consistently profitable than those relying on passive word of mouth. Burnout is most common in small operations. Referred customers were more valuable and more loyal in academic data from banking. For a side business the best fit is automated, low-touch channels:
- a referral reward paid in service credit
- partner deals (accountants, printers, coworking spaces, business-registration helpers)
- Jure's own Google Business Profile and niche SEO pages
- small batches of highly personalized "demo-first" outreach that respects EU and Slovenian e-marketing rules

In Slovenia a **s.p. is a natural person**, which matters for consent rules.

### Cited Findings
- Acquisition channels vs outcomes — [The Admin Bar 2026](https://theadminbar.com/2026-survey/):

  | | Proactive (SEO, partnerships, community) | Passive word of mouth |
  |---|---|---|
  | Over $100k revenue | 50.3% | 31.8% |
  | Consistently profitable | 53.8% | 33.1% |
  | Under $50k revenue | 26.2% | 45.6% |

- Burnout as the #1 barrier: 15.1% of businesses under $50k revenue, 15.0% at $50–99k, 8.8% at $100–199k and 3.3% at $200k+ — [The Admin Bar 2026](https://theadminbar.com/2026-survey/)
- Referral value, from Schmitt, Skiera & Van den Bulte (*Journal of Marketing*, 2011; bank customer data) — [Wharton PDF (2011)](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/04/Schmitt-Skiera-vandenBulte-2011-Referral-Programs-Customer-Value.pdf); [extended version](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2013/05/Schmitt_Skiera_VandenBulte_2013_Referrral_Programs_2.pdf) [OLDER; banking context]:
  - Referred customers had ~16% higher lifetime value over six years and churned ~18% more slowly.
  - The retention advantage did not erode over time.
- Gartner: 73% of B2B buyers actively avoid suppliers who send irrelevant outreach — [Gartner press release 2025](https://gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-sales-survey-finds-61-percent-of-b2b-buyers-prefer-a-rep-free-buying-experience) / [2026](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-03-09-gartner-sales-survey-finds-67-percent-of-b2b-buyers-prefer-a-rep-free-experience) [I could not confirm which of the two releases carries this figure]
- Cold-email benchmarks: average B2B reply rates of roughly 1–5% (2026) — [Salesmotion](https://salesmotion.io/blog/cold-outreach-best-practices) [VENDOR; approximate]
- Demo-first outreach claims (10 demos → ~1 deal) and the spam warning — [SkipThePitch](https://skipthepitch.app/resources/demo-first-web-design-outreach) [VENDOR; unverified]
- EU law, ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC, Art. 13 — [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/ALL/?uri=CELEX:32002L0058):
  - Email direct marketing needs prior consent from subscribers who are natural persons.
  - A "soft opt-in" covers similar own products sold to existing customers, provided they can object.
  - Member states decide how to protect legal persons.
- Slovenian law: a samostojni podjetnik (s.p.) is legally a *natural person* doing business (ZGD-1, Art. 3) — [PISRS, ZGD-1](http://www.pisrs.si/Pis.web/pregledPredpisa?id=ZAKO4291)

### Inferences
- **Referral mechanics (copyable).** Make the reward two-sided and paid in service credit, which costs Jure much less than cash:
  - the referrer gets e.g. 2–3 months of care free per signed client
  - the new client gets a bonus, e.g. an extra language or page

  Automate it with a unique referral link per client, tracked in Supabase, plus a "Priporočite nas" ("Recommend us") block in every monthly report. The bank study suggests referred clients stay longer, which suits a care-plan business.
- **Partner channel (low effort, high fit for Slovenia).** These businesses meet new and small businesses at the moment they need a website:
  - accountants (*računovodski servisi*)
  - SPOT points and business-registration helpers
  - print shops (business cards, signage)
  - photographers
  - coworking spaces and local business associations

  Offer partners a referral fee or white-label "website for your clients" package, plus co-branded flyers with a QR code to the configurator. The Admin Bar data links partnerships and other proactive channels to better outcomes.
- **Own SEO and Google Business Profile.** Publish niche landing pages ("spletna stran za frizerski salon cena", i.e. "hair salon website price"), a public price page (see Q5) and a Google Business Profile with reviews. These compound without evening hours.
- **Cold outreach to businesses without websites.**
  - Prefer *phone, in person or post* plus a personalized demo link over mass email.
  - Many Slovenian micro-businesses are s.p. (natural persons), so consent rules for email may apply. Have the legal researcher confirm the ZEKom-2 regime before any email campaign.
  - Keep batches small and personal; the demo-first source warns about spam, and Gartner reports that 73% of buyers avoid suppliers who send irrelevant outreach.
- **Protect against burnout.** Choose channels that don't need daytime calls. Cap monthly intake, which also gives honest scarcity. Grow recurring revenue so that new-project volume, and with it sales time, can stay modest.

### Gaps
- Slovenian specifics of the ZEKom-2 B2B cold-email rules were not researched here; that is the legal researcher's scope.
- I found no data on partner-channel performance (e.g. accountants) for web services, and no data on referral-reward size vs referral rate for small service businesses.
- The demo-first conversion numbers are a single vendor's unverified claim.

## Q8. Which features move the needle for small-business clients, which add support burden, and how should they be bundled into tiers?

### Takeaway
The features with the best evidence that *end customers* care about them are:
- speed
- mobile usability
- clear contact and click-to-call
- reviews
- online booking for appointment businesses (~two-thirds of consumers prefer booking online)
- native-language and multilingual content for tourism and export (76% prefer their own language; 40% won't buy in another)

E-shops, custom booking engines, AI chatbots, newsletters and member areas add disproportionate support burden for a solo operator. Offer them by **embedding third-party SaaS** or as **priced add-ons**, not as tier defaults.

### Cited Findings
- **Speed.** Portent (2022) found:
  - a B2B site loading in 1 second has a conversion rate ~3× higher than one loading in 5 seconds
  - average goal conversion of ~40% at 1s vs 34% at 2s
  - e-commerce conversion of 3.05% at 1s vs 0.67% at 4s

  Source: [Portent](https://www.portent.com/blog/analytics/research-site-speed-hurting-everyone.htm). Secondary write-ups give 2.5× for B2C.
- **Online booking.** 67% of consumers prefer booking appointments online over calling, emailing or walking in, up from 56% in 2021. The figure is attributed to a GetApp survey, but secondary sources disagree on whether it is the 2024 or 2025 edition — [SimplyBook.me](https://simplybook.me/blog/online-booking-statistics) [VENDOR/SECONDARY]. An older field-service survey found 44% preferred online scheduling — [EC&M (2020)](https://www.ecmweb.com/construction/article/21129444/survey-says-44-of-consumers-prefer-to-book-a-field-service-technician-online) [OLDER]
- **Multilingual.** CSA Research (8,709 consumers in 29 countries, fieldwork Nov 2019–Jan 2020): 76% of online shoppers prefer to buy products with information in their native language, and 40% will not buy from websites in other languages — [CSA Research press release](https://csa-research.com/blogs-events/csa-in-the-media/press-releases/consumers-prefer-their-own-language); [CSA "Can't Read, Won't Buy – B2C"](https://csa-research.com/Featured-Content/Global-Growth/CRWB-Series/CRWB-B2C) [OLDER]

## Q9. Pitfalls (underpricing, scope creep, custom requests, support load) and how productized operators limit them

### Takeaway
The most evidenced pitfall is **underpricing**: projects under $1k carry the highest share of rarely or never profitable businesses (37.5%) in the 2026 Admin Bar data. Burnout is concentrated in the smallest operations. Productized operators contain scope and support with:
- written scope plus an **explicit exclusions list**
- **one-request-at-a-time** queues
- **precise monthly edit caps**
- content deadlines and consolidated feedback rounds
- a priced change-request menu
- a "Custom" tier that absorbs special requests at a high price

Support hours, not hosting costs, are what breaks first, at around 25 clients per a vendor guide.

### Cited Findings
- Underpricing: the $100–999 project band shows 25.0% consistently profitable and 37.5% rarely or never profitable. The $5,000–9,999 band shows 56.8% and 2.7% — [The Admin Bar 2026](https://theadminbar.com/2026-survey/)
- Burnout as the #1 barrier: 15.1% (under $50k revenue) vs 3.3% ($200k+). Burnout was reported by 16.9% of women and 8.1% of men — [The Admin Bar 2026](https://theadminbar.com/2026-survey/)
- Hormozi: don't cut prices to win sales, because lower prices erode margins and attract clients who are never satisfied — [EcomCrew summary](https://www.ecomcrew.com/100m-offers-book-summary/) [OLDER/SECONDARY]
- Scope control at Designjoy: one request at a time, a listed set of excluded deliverables, and refunds limited to week one with completed work never refunded — [Designjoy](https://www.designjoy.co/)
- Scope control in WaaS — [We.Inc](https://we.inc/blog/website-as-a-service-subscription-websites) [VENDOR]:
  - state edit limits precisely ("up to 2 content changes a month")
  - "support hours, not platform cost, usually break first, so cap edits before reaching about 25 clients"
  - WaaS is "a poor fit for heavily custom, one-off projects"
  - add a setup fee for slow builds
- Productized definition: scope written so a stranger could check it, and "a productized service with undefined scope is a fixed-price custom engagement" — [ManyRequests](https://www.manyrequests.com/blog/productized-service-guide); [Agiled](https://agiled.app/hub/agencies/how-to-build-a-productized-service) [SECONDARY; which phrase is on which page is uncertain]

### Inferences
**Pitfall → countermeasure checklist for Jure**

| Pitfall | Countermeasure |
|---|---|
| Underpricing to "win" in a small market | Compete on speed, preview-before-pay, guarantees and included care, not on the lowest price. Low entry prices only for fully templated builds that take a few hours, always paired with a recurring plan. |
| Scope creep in builds | Fixed page and feature lists per tier; exclusions list on the pricing page and in the contract; 2 consolidated revision rounds; a priced change menu; anything else goes to "Po meri" (Custom). |
| Clients not delivering content (the classic stall) | A guided onboarding form in Supabase with required uploads; a 10-day content deadline; an AI-drafted default copy option; a launch-and-iterate policy; final invoice at launch regardless. |
| Custom requests from low-tier clients | A "from €X" Custom tier as the polite no. Keep the template system closed: no custom layouts in the lower tiers. |
| Support load growing with the client count | Monthly edit caps with no rollover; one request at a time; a request form instead of phone or WhatsApp; a published response time; plan capacity before ~25 active care clients. |
| Lock-in disputes and churn friction | Domain in the client's name; clear ownership split; buyout price inside the minimum term; easy export. This builds trust and limits support disputes. |
| Burnout in a side business | A monthly intake cap (honest scarcity); automate proposals, previews and reports; batch client work into fixed evening slots; grow recurring revenue so fewer new sales are needed. |
| Over-reliance on a few clients | Many small subscriptions; the Admin Bar data shows high client concentration correlates with lower profitability. |

### Gaps
- I found no recent quantitative data on scope-creep frequency or support-hours-per-client for productized web services. The ~25-client threshold is a single vendor's rule of thumb.
- I found no evidence specific to the Slovenian market on price sensitivity or on how small-business clients react to strict scope terms.
- The Admin Bar figures describe WordPress businesses globally and are correlational.
